The Ladder of Causality: Association, Intervention, Counterfactual

Applied AI Wiki — reference article—Updated November 6, 2026—MLAIA Data Science
Wiki Home

The ladder of causality is Judea Pearl's three-level classification of the questions that can be asked of data: association (what does seeing X tell me about Y?), intervention (what happens to Y if I do X?) and counterfactuals (what would Y have been had I acted differently?). Each rung needs more than the one below it: association needs only data, intervention needs an experiment or causal assumptions, and counterfactuals need a model of the mechanism that generated the data. The practical use of the ladder is to check which rung a question sits on before choosing a method to answer it.

What is the ladder of causality?

Pearl presented the ladder for a general audience in The Book of Why (with Dana Mackenzie), and the technical literature refers to the same idea as the causal hierarchy. Its central claim is that the three kinds of question are different in kind as well as in difficulty. Data collected on a lower rung cannot, by itself, answer a question on a higher rung, however large the dataset and however flexible the model. Something extra has to be supplied: a randomized experiment, or explicit assumptions about how the variables influence each other, usually written as a causal diagram or a structural causal model.

What does rung one, association, answer?

Question: "What does observing X tell me about Y?" Notation: the conditional probability P(y | x).

What it needs: observed data and a statistical model. No causal assumptions are required, which is why this rung is the easiest to reach and the easiest to over-interpret.

Business example: customers who contacted support twice in a month churn at a higher rate than those who did not. That is a valid basis for a churn-risk score. It does not say that preventing support contacts would reduce churn.

Clinical example: patients with a raised biomarker have a higher rate of readmission. This supports a risk prediction model. It does not say that lowering the biomarker would prevent readmission.

Methods on this rung: correlation and regression used descriptively, classification and risk scoring, forecasting, clustering, and most supervised and unsupervised machine learning, including deep learning trained on observational data.

What does rung two, intervention, answer?

Question: "What will happen to Y if I set X to a given value?" Notation: P(y | do(x)), where Pearl's do-operator marks that X is set by an action and is no longer the result of its usual causes. In general P(y | do(x)) differs from P(y | x), because the people who happen to have X differ from those who do not.

What it needs: either a randomized experiment, which implements do(x) directly, or observational data plus causal assumptions strong enough to identify the effect. Typical assumptions are that all common causes of treatment and outcome have been measured (the back-door criterion in Pearl's framework, ignorability in Rubin's potential-outcomes framework), or that a valid instrument or natural experiment exists.

Business example: "If we raise the price by five percent, what happens to demand?" Historical prices were set in response to demand, so the observed price–demand relationship is confounded. A price experiment or a credible quasi-experimental design is needed.

Clinical example: "If patients like these are given the drug, what is their risk of the outcome compared with not giving it?" This is the question a randomized controlled trial answers, and the question that observational studies try to answer through target trial emulation.

Methods on this rung: randomized controlled trials and A/B tests; covariate adjustment, matching and propensity-score methods; instrumental variables; difference-in-differences; regression discontinuity; synthetic control; and Pearl's do-calculus for deciding whether an effect is identifiable from a given diagram. Estimating how an effect varies across segments, as in uplift modeling, is also a rung-two task. These methods are described in the causal inference overview.

What does rung three, counterfactuals, answer?

Question: "Given what actually happened, what would have happened had I acted differently?" Notation: P(yx | x′, y′): the probability that Y would have been y under action x, for a case in which x′ and y′ were actually observed.

What it needs: a structural causal model, meaning functional relationships between variables together with assumptions about the unobserved factors. Pearl describes the computation in three steps: abduction (use the observed evidence to update what is known about the unobserved factors for this case), action (change the model to reflect the hypothetical intervention) and prediction (compute the outcome in the changed model). Because the same individual is never observed under both actions, counterfactual claims about individuals cannot be checked by experiment alone and rest on the model.

Business example: "This customer received the discount and renewed. Would they have renewed without it?" This is a question about one case, and it underlies attribution: how much of an observed result was because of an action.

Clinical example: "This patient was treated and recovered. Would they have recovered without treatment?" Questions of this type arise in the attribution of harm and in reasoning about individual response.

Methods on this rung: structural causal models and structural equation models; mediation analysis that separates direct from indirect effects; probabilities of necessity and sufficiency; and counterfactual definitions of fairness. Results on this rung are often reported as bounds, since point estimates require strong assumptions.

How do the three rungs compare?

RungQuestionTypical methodWhat can go wrong
1. AssociationWhat does seeing X tell me about Y?Regression, classification, forecasting, most machine learningReading a predictive pattern as an effect; confounding; selection bias; patterns that break when the policy changes
2. InterventionWhat happens to Y if I do X?Randomized experiment; adjustment, propensity scores, instrumental variables, difference-in-differencesUnmeasured confounding; an invalid instrument or failed parallel trends; underpowered experiments; interference between units
3. CounterfactualWhat would Y have been for this case had I done otherwise?Structural causal model; mediation analysis; boundsConclusions depend on untestable modeling assumptions; group-level effects presented as individual-level facts

Why can't more data move you up a rung?

More data narrows the uncertainty around P(y | x). It does not change what P(y | x) means. Two different causal structures, for example "X causes Y" and "a third factor causes both", can produce exactly the same observational distribution while implying different results under intervention. No amount of data from that distribution can tell them apart. Climbing from rung one to rung two therefore takes either control over X (an experiment) or knowledge about the structure (assumptions that can be argued for and partly tested). Climbing from rung two to rung three takes assumptions about the mechanism at the level of the individual case.

Where does machine learning sit on the ladder?

A model trained to minimize prediction error on observed data is a rung-one tool, whatever its accuracy. Its feature importances describe what the model uses to predict, which is different from what would change the outcome if altered. Machine learning still has a role on higher rungs, as a component inside a causal design: flexible models estimate propensity scores and outcome regressions, and meta-learners and causal forests estimate heterogeneous treatment effects from experimental data. In each case the causal claim comes from the design and its assumptions, and the learner supplies the estimation.

How is the ladder used in practice?

  • Classify the question first. "Who is likely to churn?" is rung one. "Will this offer reduce churn?" is rung two. "Did the offer cause this customer to stay?" is rung three.
  • Match the evidence to the rung. A rung-two decision supported only by rung-one evidence should be labelled as a hypothesis and tested.
  • State the assumptions. When an experiment is not possible, write down the causal diagram and the assumptions the estimate depends on, so that others can challenge them.
  • Be cautious with individual-level claims. Most business and clinical decisions need rung-two answers for groups. Rung-three statements about specific people need a stronger model and should carry their uncertainty.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three rungs of the ladder of causality?

The three rungs are association (seeing), intervention (doing) and counterfactuals (imagining). Association describes how variables move together in observed data, intervention describes what happens when you deliberately change a variable, and counterfactuals describe what would have happened to a specific case had a different action been taken.

Who introduced the ladder of causality?

The ladder of causality was introduced by the computer scientist Judea Pearl. It was popularized in The Book of Why, written with Dana Mackenzie, and corresponds to what the technical literature calls the causal hierarchy.

Which rung does machine learning operate on?

Standard supervised machine learning operates on the first rung, association. A model trained on observed data learns to predict an outcome from features under the conditions that produced the data. It does not, without further assumptions or experiments, say what happens when you intervene on one of those features.

What is the difference between an intervention and a counterfactual?

An intervention asks what happens to a population, or to a subgroup, if an action is taken from now on. A counterfactual asks what would have happened to a particular case, given what was actually observed for it, had a different action been taken. Interventions can be answered by randomized experiments. Counterfactuals about individuals generally also need a structural causal model.

Is an A/B test on rung two or rung three?

An A/B test is on rung two. Randomization lets you estimate the effect of an intervention on average, or within segments. It does not reveal what a specific user who saw version A would have done under version B, which is a rung-three question.

Key takeaways

  • The ladder of causality separates three kinds of question: association (seeing), intervention (doing) and counterfactuals (imagining).
  • Data from a lower rung cannot answer a higher-rung question without an experiment or explicit causal assumptions.
  • Predictive machine learning sits on rung one; experiments and identification strategies reach rung two; structural causal models are needed for rung three.
  • Classifying a question by rung before choosing a method prevents the most common error: acting on a prediction as if it were an effect.
