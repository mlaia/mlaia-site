Confounder shift: interactive demo

Confounder shift is when something linked to the disease in the study data, such as age, is linked to it differently where the model is used. A model that folded the study’s age pattern into its score loses accuracy there, and can fall below a model that ignores age. In this demo a model that adds age to the image evidence scores an AUC of 0.83 in the study and 0.64 in a population where age no longer tracks disease, while the image evidence alone scores 0.72 in both. Keeping age as a separate prior, and replacing it with the local one, brings the score back to 0.72.

In one line: Image plus age scores AUC 0.83 in the study and 0.64 where age no longer tracks disease; the image evidence alone scores 0.72 in both, and a stated risk of 50% is really 34%.

Where this matters

Any model developed on a case-control study, a referral clinic or a single site and then used in screening or at another site. Age is the usual confounder; sex, scanner, site and referral route behave the same way. Ask how the model handles each one, and test it in a population where the link is different.

What this experiment models

Each patient has two numbers that are independent given the diagnosis: an age score and an image score, both normally distributed. In the study the diseased group’s age score is higher by an amount that gives the chosen AUC for age alone. In the new population that difference is multiplied by the age link, from 100% to minus 100%. The image score differs between diseased and healthy by the same amount everywhere. Every method is a weighted sum of the two scores: the image evidence alone, a plain image model that also carries the chosen share of the study’s age pattern, the image evidence plus the study’s age prior, and the image evidence plus the age prior of the new population. AUC and the actual risk behind each stated risk are computed exactly, with no random sampling. Prevalence is 20% in both populations. The local prior is assumed known; in practice it is estimated from local age and disease rates, which needs no images.

The principle

How it works

A confounder is something that goes with the diagnosis in the data without being the disease. Age is the usual one: in many studies the patients are older than the healthy volunteers. Age then predicts the label well, and in this demo it reaches an AUC of 0.78 with no image at all. That is real information in the study. It is information about who was recruited, and it holds only where the same kind of people are tested.

There are two ways age gets into a model. It can be given as an input next to the image. Or the image itself can reveal it, because tissue changes with age, and a plain image model then learns the age pattern without being asked. Either way the score becomes a mix of evidence about the disease and a guess based on age. Where the age link is the same as in the study the mix helps. Where the link is weaker, absent or reversed, the age part adds errors, and the stated risks are too high for older patients and too low for younger ones.

The remedy is to keep the two parts separate. The image evidence is trained so that it carries no age pattern, for example by weighting or matching the study groups by age. Age enters as a prior, a starting risk, and the image evidence moves that risk up or down. The two are added on a log-odds scale. A prior can then be replaced with the one that fits the population in front of you, while the image evidence stays as it is. See clinical model validation.

Worked example

A model built on older patients and younger volunteers

Picture a team that develops an imaging model for a disease of later life. The patients come from a specialist clinic and are mostly old. The healthy volunteers are staff and relatives and are mostly younger. The model is given the image and the age, and it is tested on a held-out part of the same study.

The demo’s default settings reproduce that. Age alone separates the study groups with an AUC of 0.78 and the image evidence alone with 0.72. AUC in the study reads 0.83: adding age to the image looks like a clear gain. A plain image model that was never given the age, but picks up half of the age pattern from the image, scores 0.82 in the study.

Now the model is used for screening, where everyone is in the same age band and age says nothing about who is ill. That is the default position of the last slider. AUC here, study prior reads 0.64. AUC here, image alone reads 0.72. The model that looked best in the study is now worse than the image evidence it was built on, because it still raises the score of every older patient. The plain image model scores 0.69. The calibration plot shows the cost in risk terms: patients told 50% have an actual risk of 34%.

Move the last slider fully left, to a population where the diseased are the younger ones. AUC here, study prior reads 0.40, which is below a coin flip, and a stated risk of 50% is really 11%. The image evidence alone still reads 0.72. Move the slider fully right and every method returns to its study result.

The readout under the plots follows one older patient with a suspicious image. Age alone puts the risk at 41% in the study and 20% in the screening population. The image multiplies the odds by 1.9. The stated risk is 58% with the study prior and 32% with the local prior. Same patient, same image, and the difference is only which population the starting risk came from.

The correct practice is to decide, for each confounder, whether it is allowed to inform the score, and to make that choice visible. Train the image evidence on groups balanced for age. Report performance inside age bands as well as overall. Add age as a prior that can be replaced, and estimate that prior for each population of use from local age and disease rates.

Checklist

What to check in your own project

  • Do the diseased and healthy groups in the study differ in age, sex, site or scanner?
  • How well does the confounder alone predict the label, with no image?
  • Is performance reported inside age bands, or only overall?
  • Was the image model trained on groups balanced or weighted for the confounder?
  • Is the confounder a separate prior that can be replaced, or is it folded into one score?
  • Has the model been tested in a population where the link is different?

FAQ

Common questions

What is confounder shift in clinical AI?

It is a change, between the study and the place of use, in how something like age is linked to the disease. A model that used the study’s link keeps applying it where it no longer holds. In this demo a model that adds age to the image scores an AUC of 0.83 in the study and 0.64 in a population where age is unrelated to the disease.

Should age be an input to a diagnostic imaging model?

Age can be used, but as a separate prior and not as one more input folded into a single score. The link between age and disease in a study reflects who was recruited. Kept separate, the prior can be replaced for a new population. In the demo that takes the AUC from 0.64 back to 0.72, the level of the image evidence.

Why does my model do worse in screening than in the case-control study?

One common reason is that the study groups differed in age or another confounder and the model learned that difference. In screening the groups are alike, so that part of the score no longer helps and adds errors. In the demo the result is an AUC of 0.64 against 0.72 for the image evidence alone, and patients told 50% have an actual risk of 34%.

How do I stop an imaging model from learning age?

Balance the training groups for age, by matching diseased and healthy patients on age or by weighting them so the age distributions agree. Then check the result inside age bands: a model that ranks patients well within each band is using something other than age. No method guarantees it, so test on a population with a different age link.

How is confounder shift different from shortcut learning?

A shortcut is a feature with no medical meaning, such as a tag in the corner of the image, and the fix is to remove it. A confounder such as age carries real information about risk, so removing it throws something away. The fix is to keep it apart from the image evidence and use the value that fits each population.
