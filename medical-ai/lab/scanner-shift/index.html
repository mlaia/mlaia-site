Scanner shift in medical imaging: interactive demo

Scanner shift is the drop in performance when an imaging model meets images from a scanner, protocol or site that differs from its training data. The anatomy is the same, but contrast, brightness and noise are not. In this demo a simple lesion segmentation is tuned on synthetic images from scanner A and then applied to the same anatomy as scanner B would record it, so you can watch Dice fall as B’s contrast and noise move away from A’s.

Where this matters

Imaging models moved between scanners, protocols, vendors or sites. Learned models are far more flexible than this rule, but they can still depend on intensity and noise characteristics of the scanners they were trained on, which is why external, multi-site testing matters.

What this experiment models

A procedural slice: a body outline, two organs, a bright bony structure and an irregular lesion with a brighter core. Scanner A adds fixed Gaussian noise. Scanner B scales contrast around the image mean and adds its own noise. The “model” is a 3 × 3 mean filter followed by an intensity window, grid-searched for the best Dice on scanner A and then frozen. Normalisation, when on, z-scores each image before the window is applied, and the window is re-tuned on normalised scanner A.

The principle

How it works

The image is a procedural slice: a body outline, two organs, a bright bony structure and an irregular lesion with a brighter core. The model smooths the image with a 3 × 3 mean filter and labels every pixel inside an intensity window as lesion. The window is chosen by grid search for the best Dice on scanner A and then frozen, as a trained model’s parameters would be.

Scanner B stretches or compresses the contrast around the image mean and adds its own noise. Lower contrast pulls the lesion’s intensities out of the window and it is missed; higher contrast pushes organ tissue into it and creates false lesions; more noise scatters pixels across the window edges. The histogram shows the lesion and tissue intensities on scanner B against the window learned on A.

Normalising each image’s intensities before segmentation removes the contrast change almost completely, but not the noise. Deep networks are far more flexible than one intensity window, yet they can also depend on the intensity and noise characteristics of their training scanners, so imaging models need testing on data from other scanners and sites.
