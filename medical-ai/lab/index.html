Interactive Medical AI Lab

The fourteen demos below show, on synthetic data, how a medical AI model can pass its headline metric and still be wrong in the way that matters: beats, patients, thresholds, scanners, lesions, identifiers, slice thickness, sample size, subgroups, prevalence, shortcuts, drift and alert load. They are for clinicians who are asked to trust a model, for hospital and health-fund data teams who validate one, and for quality and regulatory people who have to sign it off. Each demo opens on a default reading you can quote:

  • Heart rate from ECG: under heavy movement the rate error is +0.7 bpm while 3 beats are false and 4 are missed.
  • Patient leakage: reported test AUC 0.98 split by record, 0.76 split by patient, 0.72 on new patients.
  • Calibration & thresholds: two models with AUC 0.845 flag 301 and 918 of every 1,000 patients at the same 10% threshold.
  • Scanner shift: Dice 0.86 on the tuning scanner, 0.01 on a scanner with 0.70× contrast and identical anatomy.
  • Dice vs clinical error: Dice 0.98 with 1 of 2 lesions found; the missed 8 mm lesion is 1.4% of the lesion area.
  • Note de-identification: default rules catch 6 of 13 identifiers in the fictional note, none of the address, towns, occupation or unique event.
  • Nodule volume in 3D: an unchanged 8 mm nodule measures 189 mm³ against a true 254 mm³ on 5 mm slices, and two scans of it can differ by 58%.
  • Sample size: 90% sensitivity measured on 20 positive cases has a 95% interval of 70% to 97%.
  • Hidden subgroup failure: overall AUC 0.87 and 90% sensitivity, but AUC 0.66 and 59% sensitivity in a subgroup that is 15% of patients.
  • Slice-by-slice segmentation in 3D: Dice 0.98 on the slices the model segments, yet one lesion is reported as 3 and its longest diameter as 20 mm instead of 30 mm.
  • Predictive value in 3D: a test that is 90% sensitive and 90% specific has a positive predictive value of 8.3% at 1% prevalence.
  • Shortcut learning: AUC 0.94 at the training hospitals and 0.64 at a new one, with 70% of the model’s weight on a hospital tag.
  • Drift over time: the alarm fires 6 months after drift begins, when the true AUC has fallen from 0.85 to 0.79.
  • Alert burden: a model with AUC 0.90 at 90% sensitivity sends a nurse 22 alerts per shift, about 1 in 199 of them real.

About the lab

What the lab is for

The Medical AI lab is a set of fourteen small experiments on the questions that decide whether a clinical model can be trusted. Was it tested on patients it had never seen? Do its predicted risks mean what they say? Does it hold up on another scanner? Does the headline metric reflect the clinical error? Is the text it uses really de-identified? Is a nodule growing, or was it measured on thicker slices? Is the test set large enough to support the claim? Does the overall score hold in every subgroup? What does a positive result mean when the condition is rare? Did the model learn the disease or the hospital? Will anyone notice when it degrades, and how many alerts will it send? Each demo isolates one of these problems, lets you change one or two settings and shows the numbers a validation report would contain.

It is written for people who build, buy or review medical AI: data scientists and machine-learning engineers in digital health and medical devices, clinical researchers who work with prediction models, and product, quality and regulatory staff who need to read a validation report and ask the right questions. It assumes no statistics beyond knowing what a test set is.

Every signal, image, patient and note is synthetic and generated in your browser. The demos are educational: they illustrate general principles, they are not medical advice and they say nothing about the performance of any real model. Each demo also has its own page with a worked example, a checklist for your own project and answers to common questions.

For the methods behind the demos, see clinical model validation, medical imaging AI, clinical data de-identification and the wiki page on medical AI validation.

Synthetic data · illustrative · not a medical device

Where this matters

Wearables, patient monitors and any algorithm that reports a rate or count from a biosignal. A validation that compares only averages can pass a detector whose individual beats are wrong.

What this experiment models

A synthetic ECG built from Gaussian P, QRS and T waves at a known, slowly drifting heart rate, plus motion artifact made of baseline wander, muscle noise and movement bursts. The detector is a classical QRS detector: zero-phase 5–15 Hz band-pass, derivative, squaring, a 150 ms moving window, an adaptive threshold and a 250 ms refractory period. Detected beats are matched to true beats within 100 ms. It is the same code as the live demo on the Medical AI page.

Where this matters

Any clinical dataset with repeated visits, admissions, images or samples per patient. The split has to follow the unit the model will meet in practice: new patients, and often new sites or later time periods.

What this experiment models

Each synthetic patient has a stable set of 8 features, a fingerprint, and a single outcome that shifts 3 of those features slightly. Each visit adds small visit-to-visit noise to the fingerprint, so a patient’s visits are much more alike than two different patients. A distance-weighted 7-nearest-neighbour classifier is scored with the area under the ROC curve (AUC). The data is fixed by a seed, so the same settings always give the same numbers.

Clinical model validation / calibration

Same ranking. Different usefulness.

What this shows: a model that ranks patients just as well (same AUC) can still give wrong risks, flag the wrong number of people and deliver less net benefit, especially at a hospital where the disease is more or less common.

Synthetic data · illustrative · not a medical device

Where this matters

Risk scores that trigger an action: a test, a referral, a treatment. Discrimination alone does not show whether the threshold makes sense, and a model moved to a new population usually needs its calibration checked again.

What this experiment models

4,000 synthetic patients with a risk score. True risk is the logistic function of the score plus an intercept set by the prevalence slider. The model is the same logistic function with the intercept that fits 10% prevalence; the miscalibrated variant multiplies its log-odds by 0.6 and adds 1, so most patients get too high a risk and the highest-risk patients too low a one. Outcomes are expected values, not random draws, so the numbers do not jitter. Net benefit follows decision-curve analysis: true positives minus false positives weighted by the threshold odds, t / (1 − t), per patient.

Medical imaging AI / domain shift

Tuned on one scanner. Tested on another.

What this shows: a segmentation rule tuned on scanner A fails on scanner B when contrast or noise changes, although the anatomy is identical. Normalising intensities first removes most of the contrast effect, not the noise.

Where this matters

Imaging models moved between scanners, protocols, vendors or sites. Learned models are far more flexible than this rule, but they can still depend on intensity and noise characteristics of the scanners they were trained on, which is why external, multi-site testing matters.

What this experiment models

A procedural slice: a body outline, two organs, a bright bony structure and an irregular lesion with a brighter core. Scanner A adds fixed Gaussian noise. Scanner B scales contrast around the image mean and adds its own noise. The “model” is a 3 × 3 mean filter followed by an intensity window, grid-searched for the best Dice on scanner A and then frozen. Normalisation, when on, z-scores each image before the window is applied, and the window is re-tuned on normalised scanner A.

Medical imaging AI / evaluation metrics

A high Dice. A missed lesion.

What this shows: Dice is dominated by the largest structure, so missing a small lesion barely moves it. A boundary distance and a simple count of lesions found tell a different story.

Open as a page: Dice score vs clinical error →

Change the prediction

Two true lesions: one about 68 mm across and one small. Shrink the small lesion below about 5 mm and even the 95th-percentile distance stops noticing that it was missed.

Synthetic data · illustrative · not a medical device

Where this matters

Segmentation and detection where small findings change the clinical decision: metastases, small nodules, early lesions. Report per-lesion detection and a boundary metric next to Dice, and choose metrics for the clinical question.

What this experiment models

Binary masks on a 160 × 160 grid with 1 mm pixels. The large true lesion is a slightly irregular disc; the prediction follows its boundary with a fixed wobble scaled by the roughness slider. Dice is 2|A∩B| / (|A| + |B|) over all pixels. Boundary distances are measured between the boundary pixels of the two masks in both directions; HD95 is the larger of the two directed 95th percentiles, and the maximum is the Hausdorff distance. A lesion counts as detected if any predicted pixel overlaps it.

    Where this matters

    Free-text notes, letters and reports before they reach analysts, vendors or language models. Measure missed identifiers and over-redaction on your own notes and languages; de-identification lowers disclosure risk but is not a guarantee of anonymity.

    What this experiment models

    One fictional note in three versions, with a ground-truth annotation of 13 identifiers: names, dates, an ID number and a phone number (all zeros), an address and towns, and two rare details. Real systems combine rules with trained models and human review.

    Medical imaging AI / 3D measurement

    Same nodule. Different volume.

    What this shows: the volume a CT segmentation reports depends on the slice thickness and on where the slices happen to fall. On thick slices, two scans of a nodule that has not changed can differ by more than the change used to call growth.

    Open as a page: Nodule volume and slice thickness →

    Change the scan

    Slice position shifts the slices along the scan axis by a share of one slice, as happens when the patient lies a little differently. The nodule itself never changes. In-plane pixels are 0.7 mm, and a voxel is kept when at least half of it is nodule.

    Where this matters

    Follow-up of lung nodules, lesions and any structure whose growth is judged from its volume. Baseline and follow-up need the same slice thickness and reconstruction, and a growth call should be compared with the measurement variability expected at that size. The 25% figure is the volume change the British Thoracic Society guideline on pulmonary nodules (2015) uses to define significant growth.

    What this experiment models

    One slightly lobulated synthetic nodule, the same shape at every diameter, sampled on a fine grid. A scan divides space into voxels of 0.7 × 0.7 mm in the slice plane and the chosen thickness along the scan axis. Each voxel holds the share of its volume that is nodule, which is the partial-volume effect, and the segmentation keeps voxels that are at least half nodule. Measured volume is the number of kept voxels times the voxel volume. The largest gap between two scans is the highest measured volume over the lowest across twenty slice positions. Real scanners add reconstruction kernels, noise and motion, and real segmentations differ; the demo isolates slice thickness and position only. The 3D view is drawn on a plain canvas and needs no plug-in.

    Where this matters

    Any validation study, external test or post-market check that reports sensitivity, specificity or another proportion. Plan the number of positive cases before collecting data, report every estimate with its interval, and state the minimum acceptable value in advance.

    What this experiment models

    The validation set has the chosen number of patients; the number with the condition is that number times the prevalence, rounded. Patients for ±5 points is the number of positive cases at which the interval half-width falls to 5 percentage points, divided by the prevalence.

    09

    Clinical model validation / subgroups

    Good overall. Weak for some.

    What this shows: a model can score well on the whole test set and still fail one group of patients, because a small group barely moves the overall figure. Only a per-subgroup report shows it.

    Open as a page: Hidden subgroup failure →

    Change the subgroup

    The subgroup can be an age band, a site, a device or any group the training data covered poorly. Signal is how well the model separates cases from non-cases there, relative to the main group. One threshold is used for everyone, set to reach the target sensitivity on the whole test set.

    One model, three views

    Overall AUC—

    Synthetic data · illustrative · not a medical device

    Where this matters

    Models used across age bands, sexes, sites, devices, disease stages or care settings. Decide the subgroups before looking at the results, report discrimination and error rates for each with its interval, and check that each subgroup holds enough cases to say anything.

    What this experiment models

    6,000 synthetic patients, 20% with the outcome, each assigned to the main group or the subgroup. The model’s score is a standard normal value, shifted up by two standard deviations for cases in the main group and by the chosen share of that for cases in the subgroup. Prevalence is the same in both groups. The threshold is the highest cut-off that still reaches the target sensitivity on the whole test set, and it is applied to everyone. AUC, sensitivity and specificity are then computed for the whole set and for each group, with a 95% Wilson interval for the subgroup sensitivity. The data is fixed by a seed, so the same settings always give the same numbers.

    Medical imaging AI / 3D segmentation

    Each slice fine. The lesion in pieces.

    What this shows: a model that segments a 3D lesion one slice at a time can score well on every slice it segments and still miss the lesion in a few slices. The lesion then comes out as several separate pieces, with the wrong count, length and volume.

    Open as a page: Slice-by-slice segmentation of a 3D lesion →

    Change the prediction

    The lesion is cut into 2 mm slices. The model looks at one slice at a time, so nothing ties its answer in one slice to the next. Missed slices are ones where it finds no lesion at all.

    Where this matters

    CT and MRI segmentation used for lesion counts, longest-diameter measurements or volumes. Evaluate in 3D: report volume-level overlap, the number of connected lesions against the reference, and the measurements derived from the mask, not only a per-slice score.

    What this experiment models

    One elongated synthetic lesion, defined by its outline in each 2 mm slice. Lesions reported is the number of runs of consecutive slices with a prediction. Longest diameter is the largest extent of the largest piece, along the scan axis or within a slice, against the true lesion length.

    Clinical model validation / predictive value

    Accurate test. Mostly false positives.

    What this shows: what a positive result means depends on how common the condition is. A test with high sensitivity and specificity still produces mostly false positives when the condition is rare.

    Open as a page: Predictive value and prevalence →

    Set the test and the population

    Prevalence is the share of the tested population that has the condition. It is low in screening and higher in a referred or symptomatic group, so the same model has a different predictive value in each.

    Where this matters

    Screening, triage and any model moved from an enriched study to routine use. Report predictive values at the prevalence of the intended population, and say how many people are flagged for each real case.

    What this experiment models

    Positive predictive value is the share of positive results that are true: prevalence × sensitivity divided by all positives, which also include (1 − prevalence) × (1 − specificity) false positives.     Medical imaging AI / shortcut learning

    It learned the hospital, not the disease.

    What this shows: when something unrelated to the disease, such as a hospital tag in the corner of the image, goes with the diagnosis in the training data, a model can learn the tag. It tests well where the tag still goes with the disease and fails where it does not.

    Open as a page: Shortcut learning in medical imaging →

    Change the training data

    At 50% the tag says nothing about the diagnosis. At 90%, nine in ten diseased images carry it and nine in ten healthy images do not, as when most sick patients are imaged on one portable machine. At the new hospital the tag is unrelated to disease.

    Where this matters

    Imaging and signal models trained on data where acquisition differs by diagnosis: portable versus fixed machines, different sites for sicker patients, burned-in text, markers, tubes or rulers. Test on sites where those links are broken, and check what the model responds to.

    What this experiment models

    Each synthetic image is reduced to two inputs: a disease signal, a number that is higher on average in diseased cases by the chosen strength, and a tag that is present or absent. Half the images are diseased. In the training set and the same-hospital test set the tag is present in the chosen share of diseased images and absent in the same share of healthy ones; in the new-hospital test set it is present in half of each. The model is a logistic regression on the two inputs. Weight on the tag is the tag’s share of the two standardised weights. The schematic scan is an illustration of that share and is not itself classified.

    13

    Clinical model validation / monitoring

    It worked at launch. Then it slipped.

    What this shows: a deployed model can lose performance slowly as patients, practice or equipment change. A monitoring rule based on a few labelled cases a month either notices late or fires by chance.

    Open as a page: Model drift and monitoring →

    Set the monitoring

    The model’s true AUC is 0.85 for the first 6 months and then falls by the amount you set each month. One in five labelled cases is positive. The alarm fires when the measured AUC is below the alarm line two months in a row.

    Synthetic data · illustrative · not a medical device

    Where this matters

    Any model in routine use. Decide before deployment which metric is tracked, how labels will be obtained, how many cases a month that gives, and what margin and rule trigger a review. Watch inputs and case mix as well, because they move before labelled outcomes arrive.

    What this experiment models

    The model’s score separates cases from non-cases by an amount that gives a true AUC of 0.85, reduced month by month once drift starts, with a floor of 0.5. Each month the chosen number of labelled cases is drawn with 20% positives and the AUC is measured on them. The alarm line is the baseline AUC minus the margin, and the alarm fires at the second of two consecutive months below it. The random draws are fixed by a seed, so the same settings always give the same chart; a different seed would move the alarm by a month or two.

    Clinical model validation / workload

    A good AUC. An alert every half hour.

    What this shows: a threshold is also a workload. Counted per nurse per shift, a model with a strong AUC can raise an alert every few minutes, almost all of them false, because real events are rare and every patient is scored many times.

    Open as a page: Alert burden of an early-warning model →

    Set the model and the ward

    A shift is 12 hours. A real event, such as deterioration, happens in 2% of patient-shifts and is present at one of that patient’s scores.

    Where this matters

    Early-warning scores, sepsis and deterioration alerts, and any model whose output interrupts a clinician. Express the threshold as alerts per clinician per shift and real events per alert, and agree what load the ward can act on before choosing it.

    What this experiment models

    Scores are normally distributed for assessments with and without an event, separated by the amount that gives the chosen AUC. The threshold is the one that reaches the chosen sensitivity. Each patient is scored at regular intervals through a 12-hour shift; 2% of patient-shifts contain one real event, present at one of those scores. Alerts per shift is the number of scores times the share that exceed the threshold. The chart simulates every score for one nurse over seven shifts with a fixed seed. Repeated alerts on the same patient are counted separately, as they are when each one has to be acknowledged.
