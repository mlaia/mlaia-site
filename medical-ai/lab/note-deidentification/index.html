De-identifying a clinical note: interactive demo

De-identifying a clinical note means finding and removing or replacing the details that could identify the patient: names, dates, ID numbers, contact details, addresses and rare personal details. This demo runs a simple rule-based detector over a fully fictional discharge note in English, Hebrew or mixed Hebrew-English and scores it against a hand-made annotation, so you can see which identifiers are caught, which are missed and what is removed by mistake.

    Where this matters

    Free-text notes, letters and reports before they reach analysts, vendors or language models. Measure missed identifiers and over-redaction on your own notes and languages; de-identification lowers disclosure risk but is not a guarantee of anonymity.

    What this experiment models

    One fictional note in three versions, with a ground-truth annotation of 13 identifiers: names, dates, an ID number and a phone number (all zeros), an address and towns, and two rare details. The detector is a list of regular expressions and word lists, switched on in five levels. An identifier counts as caught when at least half of its characters are redacted; a redaction that touches no identifier counts as over-redaction. Real systems combine rules with trained models and human review.

    The principle

    How it works

    The note contains 13 annotated identifiers: four names, two dates, an ID number and a phone number (both all zeros), a street address, two towns, an occupation and a unique event. The detector is a stack of regular expressions and word lists in five levels: titled names and ID numbers; then phone numbers, numeric dates and a short list of first names; then month-name dates, addresses, towns and capitalised words; then any number and a longer name list; and finally occupations and every capitalised word.

    Names and ID numbers are caught early. Dates written in words, addresses and towns need more rules. Hebrew has no capital letters, so a name without a title is missed until it is on a list, and the list then also removes ordinary words that double as names, such as the Hebrew word for age. The occupation is caught only at the strictest level, the unique event never is, and doses, lab values and drug names are over-redacted along the way.

    Lists of identifier types, such as the HIPAA Safe Harbor list, are a starting point. Measure missed identifiers and over-redaction on your own notes and languages, and keep in mind that de-identification lowers disclosure risk but does not guarantee anonymity.
