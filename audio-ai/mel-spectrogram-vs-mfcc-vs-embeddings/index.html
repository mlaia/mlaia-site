Audio & Acoustics · Comparison

Mel spectrogram vs MFCC
vs pretrained audio embeddings.

Short answer: For sound event detection and audio classification with a neural network, the log-mel spectrogram is the default input: it keeps the time-frequency structure that convolutional and transformer models learn from. MFCCs compress each frame into a few coefficients, which suits small classical models and very tight memory, but they throw away detail a network could have used. Pretrained audio embeddings are usually the strongest choice when labelled data is small, because a model trained on a large audio collection has already learned general sound features; the price is a larger model on the device or a server. Choose by the amount of labelled data first, then by the compute budget.

A guide for teams building detectors for alarms, glass breaks, machine states, coughs or other target sounds. We compare the three inputs on what they keep, what they cost and how much labelled data they need.

When each one wins

Pick by how much labelled data you have.
Then by where the model runs.

Many pretrained audio models take log-mel input themselves, so the three options are stages of one pipeline as much as rivals. The question is how much of that pipeline you train and how much you ship.

01

Choose log-mel when

You have thousands of labelled examples or can generate them by augmentation, the model is a CNN or CRNN, and it must run on a small processor. It is also the safe starting point when nothing else is known.

02

Choose MFCCs when

The classifier is a small classical model, memory is counted in kilobytes, or an existing fixed-point front-end already produces them. For speech-like tasks with a tiny model they remain a reasonable baseline.

03

Choose pretrained embeddings when

Labels are few, classes are everyday sounds close to the pretraining data, and the platform can run the embedding model. A common route to the edge is to use the large model as a teacher or labeller and distil into a small log-mel network.

How to choose

Start from the data you have.
End on the hardware you ship.

A decision list for choosing the input before the model architecture is fixed.

01

Count labelled examples per class

With only a few hundred or fewer, try pretrained embeddings with a simple classifier first. It gives a fast baseline and shows whether the classes are separable at all.

02

Check the signal against the pretraining domain

Sample rate, frequency range and sound type must match. Ultrasonic content, vibration signals and very narrow tones may need log-mel or a linear spectrogram trained on your own data.

03

Set the device budget

Flash, RAM and cycles per hop decide whether an embedding model can ship. If not, plan for a compact log-mel model, or MFCCs with a classical classifier on the smallest parts.

04

Keep features identical in training and on the device

Window, FFT size, band edges, log offset and normalization must match numerically. A mismatch here is a frequent cause of a model that works in the notebook and fails in the product.

05

Compare on one held-out set

Evaluate the candidates on recordings from the target microphone, with event-based metrics and false alarms per hour, not only clip accuracy.

Before we start

Good questions. Straight answers.

What is the difference between a mel spectrogram and MFCCs?

A mel spectrogram is the short-time power spectrum grouped into mel-spaced frequency bands, usually followed by a log. MFCCs go one step further: a discrete cosine transform of those log energies, keeping only the first coefficients. MFCCs are therefore a compressed, decorrelated summary of the log-mel spectrum.

Are MFCCs obsolete for sound event detection?

Not obsolete, but rarely the first choice with neural networks. Networks generally do better on log-mel input, where the detail is still present. MFCCs remain useful with small classical models and on very constrained hardware.

How many mel bands should we use?

Common choices are 40 bands for small on-device models and 64 to 128 for larger ones. More bands keep more frequency detail and cost more compute and memory. The right number depends on the sounds: tonal, narrow-band events need finer resolution than short impacts.

Can pretrained audio embeddings run on a microcontroller?

Usually not directly, because the embedding models are too large. Practical options are to run the embedding model on a phone or gateway, or to distil it into a small model that takes log-mel input. Our on-device audio AI page covers the budgets involved.

Should we train on raw waveforms instead?

It is possible, and some large models do. Learned front-ends need more data and compute to match what a mel filterbank gives for free, so for small and medium datasets log-mel input is the more reliable choice.
