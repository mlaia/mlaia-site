Deep learning vs
classical DSP noise suppression.

Short answer: Classical noise suppression (spectral subtraction, Wiener filtering, adaptive filters) estimates the noise from the signal itself and removes it with very little code, memory and delay. It behaves predictably, but it assumes the noise changes slowly, so keyboard clicks, babble and other fast-changing sounds get through. Neural noise suppression learns what speech and noise sound like from large datasets and handles changing noise much better, at the cost of more compute, a training pipeline and less predictable behaviour on sounds unlike its training data. Algorithmic delay is set mainly by frame length and look-ahead, not by which family you pick. Many shipping designs are hybrids: classical processing where there is a physical model or a reference signal, and a small network for the decisions a rule can’t make.

A guide for audio and embedded teams choosing a single-channel noise suppressor for headsets, conferencing devices, intercoms or industrial communication. We compare the two families on delay, cost, robustness and how they fail.

Side by side

Two ways to estimate the noise.
One modelled, one learned.

Both families usually work in the same place: a short-time Fourier transform, a gain for each time-frequency bin, and an inverse transform. They differ in where the gain comes from.

When each one wins

Pick by how the noise behaves.
Then by what the processor allows.

The deciding questions are how fast the noise changes, how much compute each frame can spend, and how predictable the product must be.

01

Choose classical DSP when

The noise is mostly steady, the processor is a small DSP or microcontroller, or the product needs behaviour that can be explained and bounded, as in hearing, safety or intercom devices. An adaptive filter is the right tool whenever a clean reference of the noise or the echo is available.

02

Choose deep learning when

The noise changes quickly (typing, babble, machinery that starts and stops), the platform can run a quantized network on every hop, and the team can maintain a training set and an evaluation suite.

03

Use a hybrid when

Compute is limited but classical quality isn’t enough. Common patterns: a small network predicts band gains or the noise estimate and a classical filter applies them; a linear echo canceller or a beamformer runs first and a network cleans the residual; a classical gain floor limits how much the network may remove.

How to choose

Set the budgets first.
Then compare on one test set.

A decision list we’d work through before committing to an architecture.

01

Describe the noise

Record the product’s real conditions and sort the noise into steady and fast-changing. If nearly all of it is steady, a well-tuned classical suppressor may be enough.

02

Fix the delay budget

A call, a hearing device and a speech recognizer tolerate different delay. The budget sets frame length and look-ahead for both families.

03

Fix the compute budget

Know the cycles and memory available per hop on the target processor, after the rest of the audio path has taken its share.

04

Check for a reference

If the device has a loudspeaker signal, a second microphone or a vibration sensor, use adaptive filtering or beamforming for what it can remove before any single-channel suppressor.

05

Prototype both, measure the same way

Run a classical baseline and a neural candidate through one evaluation: objective quality and intelligibility scores, word error rate if a recognizer listens, and listening tests on noise held out from training.

Before we start

Good questions. Straight answers.

Is deep learning always better than classical noise suppression?

No. On fast-changing noise a well-trained network usually sounds clearly better. On steady noise the difference is small, and a classical suppressor costs far less and behaves more predictably. The answer depends on the noise, the processor and how much unpredictability the product can accept.

Does neural noise suppression add more latency than classical DSP?

Not necessarily. In both, algorithmic delay comes mainly from the analysis frame and any look-ahead. A causal neural model with the same frame has the same algorithmic delay, provided inference finishes within each hop. Models that use future frames trade delay for quality.

Can a neural noise suppressor run on a microcontroller?

Small ones can. Hybrid designs that predict a few dozen band gains need little compute and have run on embedded processors for years. Larger full-band models usually need a faster core or a neural accelerator. Our on-device audio AI page covers the constraints.

What is musical noise?

It is the artefact most associated with spectral subtraction: isolated time-frequency bins survive the subtraction at random and are heard as short, watery tones. Smoothing the gain over time, a spectral floor and decision-directed estimation of the signal-to-noise ratio all reduce it. Our spectral subtraction demo lets you hear it.

How do we test robustness to unseen noise?

Keep whole noise types, rooms, talkers and devices out of training and report results on those separately. Add recordings from the real product, including conditions with no speech at all, and listen to the worst cases instead of only averaging scores.
