Microphone array beamforming simulator

Beamforming combines the signals of several microphones so that sound from one direction adds up in phase and sound from other directions partly cancels. This simulator steers an ideal delay-and-sum linear array toward one of two tones and plots its directional response, so you can see and hear how the steering angle and the number of microphones change what gets through.

Where this matters

Microphone arrays, directional sensing and audio capture on devices. Geometry, frequency, reverberation and compute budgets shape real-world performance.

What this experiment models

An ideal uniform linear delay-and-sum array in the far field, with sound speed 343 m/s and equal source amplitudes. The chart uses the normalized complex array factor. Playback applies that factor’s gain and phase to each synthetic tone. It models two steady tones, not speech, moving sources, reverberation, microphone mismatch or an actual hardware recording.

The principle

How it works

A source off to the side reaches each microphone at a slightly different time. A delay-and-sum beamformer delays each channel to undo that arrival-time difference for the chosen look direction, then averages the channels. Sound from the look direction adds coherently; sound from other directions arrives with mismatched phases and is attenuated by the amount the array factor in the chart shows.

The microphones here are 4 cm apart. Adding microphones widens the aperture, which narrows the main lobe and deepens the attenuation away from it. The response also depends on frequency: the two sources are tones at 1,000 Hz and 650 Hz, and the lower tone sees a wider beam from the same array. Side lobes mean an off-axis source can still leak through at some angles.

Real arrays add reverberation, microphone mismatch, moving talkers and a compute budget, which is where adaptive beamformers and learned spatial filters come in. See microphone arrays and beamforming.
