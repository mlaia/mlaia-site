Output SNR compares filtered signal power with filtered noise power. Signal retained shows output signal power relative to the clean input; listen for distortion as well as noise reduction.

Where this matters

Sensor conditioning, speech front ends and embedded audio. Filtering is a useful baseline; separating overlapping speech and noise calls for more advanced methods.

What this experiment models

A deterministic four-second harmonic melody at 24 kHz, with white noise scaled to the chosen input SNR, processed by a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter. This is classical DSP, not a trained speech-enhancement model.

Negative dB means attenuation; positive dB means reinforcement. Exact cancellation produces silence in this ideal simulation.

Why the engineering matters

Real ANC depends on microphone and speaker placement, acoustic transfer paths, timing and stability. Cancellation at one point does not imply a quiet room; moving the listener or changing the sound changes the problem.

What this experiment models

Linear superposition of two steady 180 Hz tones at one observation point. Residual amplitude relative to the original is the magnitude of 1 + a·exp(jφ), where a is the amplitude ratio and φ is the phase offset. Playback uses the actual summed waveform with the same gain as the original. The phase and amplitude are set manually: this illustrates the cancellation principle, not a feedback controller, adaptive ANC algorithm or measured headphone performance. Graphs use normalized amplitude. All sounds are synthesized in the browser.

mg = thousandths of g; the 1× line is a peak amplitude. The outer-race line is the envelope-spectrum peak within ±2% of the expected frequency, in dB above the median envelope floor; noise alone reaches roughly +7 to +10 dB. The 1× alarm compares against the healthy 20 mg baseline.

Where it’s used

Predictive maintenance on motors, pumps, fans and gearboxes. See vibration analysis and diagnostics.

What this experiment models

One second of synthetic acceleration at 16,384 Hz (1 Hz FFT resolution): shaft harmonics at 1×, 2× and 3×, Gaussian sensor noise (60 mg RMS) and, for the defect, decaying 3 kHz impulses repeating at the ball-pass frequency of the outer race (BPFO = 4.5 × (1 − d/D) × shaft rate for a nine-ball bearing with d/D ≈ 0.20, about 3.59× shaft speed) with about 1% random slip. The raw spectrum is a Hann-windowed FFT scaled to peak amplitude. The envelope is the magnitude of the analytic signal of the 2–4.5 kHz band (FFT band-pass), followed by a Hann-windowed FFT. Shaft speed is assumed known, as from a tachometer. Playback is the same synthetic signal, four seconds long. Real machines add speed variation, transmission paths, mounting resonances and other faults; thresholds should come from a machine’s own baseline data.

Where it’s used

Always-on detectors on phones, wearables and sensors, often as the cheap first stage before a model. See on-device audio AI.

What this experiment models

An 8-second, 16 kHz synthetic scene: pink background noise at the chosen RMS level (dBFS before playback volume), four damped multi-resonance knocks, four 2.6 kHz beeps and two band-limited low-frequency rumbles. Each 512-sample Hann-windowed frame (16 ms hop) gives a log energy, either over the whole band or over 1–6 kHz. The floor is the median over the clip. Frames above floor + threshold are merged (gaps up to 3 frames, minimum 2 frames). A target counts as detected if any detection overlaps it; a detection that overlaps no target is a false alarm. This is a classical energy detector, not a trained model; learned detectors replace the feature and threshold, but the hit/false-alarm tradeoff remains.

Speech distortion is the power of the change to the clean component, relative to the clean signal; lower is better. This is noise reduction on a recorded signal, not ANC: ANC plays anti-noise into the air (see experiment 03).

Where it’s used

Speech front ends for calls, hearing devices and speech recognition. See speech enhancement and noise reduction.

What this experiment models

A 4.5-second, 16 kHz speech-like signal synthesized from harmonics with gliding pitch and vowel formants, plus short fricative bursts; it is not recorded speech. Noise is scaled to the chosen SNR over the speech section. The first 0.5 s contains noise only and is used to estimate the noise power spectrum. Each 512-sample Hann-windowed frame (50% overlap) gets the gain √max(1 − α·N̂/|X|², 0), clamped at the spectral floor, followed by overlap-add resynthesis. SNR and distortion are measured by applying the same gains to the clean and noise components separately. With fluctuating noise the fixed estimate goes stale, which is where adaptive estimators and trained models help.
