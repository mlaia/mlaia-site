Getting the question right

Most churn models fail before training.
At the label and the data.

The hard part is rarely the algorithm. It’s defining churn, choosing the date you predict from, and keeping the future out of the training data.

Our interactive explorer shows a churn-style prediction on synthetic customers, with what-if scenarios, SHAP explanations and a decision threshold. For a related client project, read our account of lead prioritization for one of Israel’s largest communication companies.

01

Label definition

Contractual churn has a cancellation date. Non-contractual churn needs a rule, such as no purchase in 90 days. The rule changes who counts as churned, so we set it with the team that acts on it.

02

Leakage

Data recorded after the prediction date makes a model look excellent in testing and useless in production. A logged cancellation call is a classic example. We build features as of a fixed cut-off date. See Kaufman et al. on leakage.

03

Survival framing

Often the question is when a customer leaves, not only whether. Survival models handle customers who haven’t left yet without treating them as stayers, and give risk over any horizon. Our survival analysis guide covers when to use them.

From score to action

A risk score is a ranking.
Acting on it well takes more.

A model that ranks customers well can still lead to poor decisions. These are the parts we work through with your team.

Calibration

If the model says 30%, about 30 in 100 such customers should leave. We check this on held-out customers and correct it when needed, so scores can feed cost and capacity plans. See the scikit-learn calibration guide.

Thresholds vs team capacity

If your team can contact only so many customers a week, the question is which ones. We set the cut-off from capacity and the cost of a contact, and report precision at that cut-off, not only AUC.

Explanations

SHAP values show which factors drive each score. They help the team act and catch data problems. They describe the model, not what causes churn.

Risk is not treatment effect

A high-risk customer may leave whatever you do. Another may stay anyway. Comparing customers who got an offer with those who didn’t mixes the offer’s effect with who was chosen.

Uplift modeling

Uplift models estimate how much an offer changes each customer’s chance of leaving. They need data where the offer was randomized, or a credible causal design. See causal inference & uplift.

Monitoring

Customer mix, pricing and products change. We monitor input drift, calibration and actual churn by segment, and retrain on a schedule agreed with your team.

What we measure

Measured on future customers.
At the cut-off you’ll use.

We agree the measures before modeling, and test on customers from a later period than the training data.

Ranking quality

AUC and precision-recall on a later period. Precision at the number of customers your team can contact matters more than overall AUC.

Calibration

Calibration curves and Brier score, overall and by segment, so a 30% score means roughly 30%.

Lift by decile

How much more churn sits in the top-scored groups than in a random selection. It’s easy for a retention team to read.

Incremental effect

In a holdout or A/B test, how much churn fell among customers who got the offer, compared with similar customers who didn’t. This is the number that justifies the program.

Achievable results depend on your customers, data and how churn is defined. We measure them on your own history next to your current approach. We don’t promise benchmark figures.

Before we start

Good questions. Straight answers.

How accurate will the churn model be?

We can’t say before seeing your data. We test on customers from a later period than training, next to your current approach, and report precision at the number of customers your team can contact.

Why does our model score well in testing but not in production?

Common causes are leakage, a random split instead of a time-based one, and a churn label that differs between training and use. We check each one in the data audit.

Should we contact the customers with the highest risk?

Not always. Some high-risk customers will leave whatever you do, and for some, the contact itself prompts them to leave. Risk tells you who might leave. An experiment or uplift model tells you whom an offer helps.

Do we need an A/B test?

To measure what a retention offer changes, a randomized holdout is the most reliable evidence. Without one, causal designs on historical data can help, but they rest on stronger assumptions. We state them.

What data do we need?

Dated customer history from your CRM, billing and product usage, plus records of past retention actions if you have them. For the first call, a column list is enough. Please don’t send confidential data.
