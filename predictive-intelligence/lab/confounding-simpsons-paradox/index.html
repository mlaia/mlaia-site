Simpson’s paradox: why does the effect reverse?

The effect reverses because a hidden variable, a confounder, drives both who gets the action and the outcome, so the whole dataset compares different kinds of customer while each segment compares like with like. In this demo a success-team call raises renewal by +7.6 pts among at-risk accounts and +7.1 pts among healthy ones, yet across all accounts the difference between called and uncalled accounts reads −15.8 pts, because the calls go mostly to accounts that were already at risk. The within-segment figures are the ones that answer what the call did.

Where this matters

Any action aimed at the cases that need it most: retention calls, discounts for price-sensitive customers, escalations for difficult tickets, maintenance for the oldest machines. A dashboard that compares treated with untreated then measures who was chosen as much as what the action did.

What this experiment models

20,000 synthetic accounts; half are at risk. Without a call, 80% of healthy and 40% of at-risk accounts renew; a call adds 8 percentage points in both segments. The slider sets how strongly calls depend on the segment: at 0% every account has a 50% chance of a call, at 100% it is 90% for at-risk and 10% for healthy accounts. The adjusted figure is the within-segment difference averaged over the two segments by size. Each account keeps the same random draws at every slider position, and the data is fixed by a seed, so the same settings always give the same numbers.

The principle

How it works

What the demo shows

The demo generates 20,000 synthetic accounts. Half are at risk of leaving, and that is the hidden variable. In the simulation a call from the success team raises the chance of renewal by 8 percentage points in both segments, so the true effect is +8.0 pts. At-risk accounts renew far less often than healthy ones, called or not.

At the default setting 80% of at-risk accounts and 20% of healthy accounts get a call. Compare all accounts together and the called group renews at 56.1% against 71.9% for the rest: a difference of −15.8 pts, which reads as the call doing harm. Compare within each segment and the sign reverses: +7.6 pts among at-risk accounts and +7.1 pts among healthy ones. Averaging the two segments by size gives +7.3 pts, close to the true +8.0 pts; the remaining gap is sampling noise.

Neither calculation is wrong. The pooled comparison answers a different question: 79% of called accounts are at risk, against 20% of the others, so it mostly compares at-risk accounts with healthy ones. Move the slider to 0% and calls no longer depend on the segment; the pooled difference returns to roughly the true effect.

Why it matters for a decision

The pooled number is what a dashboard shows by default: renewal for accounts that were contacted against accounts that were not. Read at face value, it says to stop calling. The same pattern appears whenever an action is aimed at the cases that need it most, and the action then looks useless or harmful because of who receives it.

What to check in your own data

  • How was the action assigned? Write down the rule or the judgement people used. Any input to that decision that also affects the outcome is a candidate confounder.
  • Is that variable recorded? If it is, compare within its levels and see whether the estimate moves. If it lived only in someone’s head, no adjustment can recover it.
  • Does the mix differ between the groups? A table of treated and untreated units by segment often shows the problem before any modelling.
  • Can part of the action be randomised? Holding out a random share of eligible accounts gives a comparison that no hidden variable can distort.

Stratifying is not always right. Adjusting for something the action itself changes removes part of the effect. The causal structure, not the data, says which variables to adjust for. For how this is handled in a project, see causal inference consulting; for background, the causal inference guide.

FAQ

Common questions

What is Simpson’s paradox in simple terms?

It is a reversal: a trend that holds in every subgroup of the data points the other way when the subgroups are combined. It arises when the groups being compared contain different mixes of subgroups, and the subgroups have different baseline outcomes.

Which is correct, the pooled result or the stratified result?

It depends on the causal structure. When the subgroup variable influences both the action and the outcome, as account risk does in this demo, the within-subgroup comparison reflects the action’s effect. When the variable is a consequence of the action, adjusting for it is the mistake.

What is a confounder?

A confounder is a variable that affects both whether a unit receives an action and the outcome being measured. It creates an association between action and outcome that the action did not cause.

How do I detect confounding in my own data?

Data alone cannot prove its absence. Start from how the action was assigned, list what influenced that decision, check whether those factors differ between the treated and untreated groups, and see whether the estimate changes when you adjust for them. A randomised holdout is the one check that does not depend on having measured everything.

Why does my retention call look harmful in the dashboard when it helps every customer?

Because the call is aimed at accounts that were already likely to leave. The called group then contains more at-risk accounts than the uncalled group, and its lower renewal rate reflects who was called, not what the call did. In the demo the pooled comparison reads −15.8 pts while the call adds about +7.3 pts within segments.

Is Simpson’s paradox the same thing as confounding?

It is the most visible form of it. A third variable, here account risk, influences both who receives the action and the outcome, and leaves an association between them that the action did not cause. When that association is strong enough to flip the sign, it is called Simpson’s paradox. Stratifying by the variable removes it, provided the variable is recorded.

Can Simpson’s paradox happen with more than two groups?

Yes. Any number of subgroups with different baseline rates and different treatment shares can produce it, and the reversal can hide inside a dashboard that slices by one dimension while the confounder is another. The fix is the same: find out how the action was assigned and compare like with like.
