Prophet vs LightGBM
for demand forecasting.

Prophet fits one model per series from trend, yearly and weekly seasonality and holidays, so it gives a reasonable, explainable forecast from a few seasons of history with very little feature work. LightGBM is a gradient-boosted tree model, usually trained as one global model across many items with lag, calendar, price and promotion features; with enough history and related series, it learns driver effects and cross-item patterns that a per-series model cannot. Use Prophet for a handful of strongly seasonal, aggregate series, LightGBM for item-level demand at scale, and backtest both against a simple baseline before choosing.

A guide for planning and data teams choosing a forecasting approach. We compare the two on what they need, what they cost to run, how they express uncertainty and how they fail.

Prophet treats each series as trend plus seasonality plus holidays. LightGBM treats forecasting as a regression on features, and is usually trained across many series at once. That difference drives most of the rows below.

This compares general properties of the two model families. Which one is more accurate depends on your items, history, drivers and horizon, and is settled by a backtest on your own data.

When each one wins

Few seasonal series, or many related items.
And sometimes neither.

Both are reasonable defaults in the right setting. Neither is the right default for every item in a catalogue.

01

Choose Prophet when

You forecast a small number of aggregate series with strong yearly and weekly patterns and known holidays, history is short, and planners need to see why the forecast moved. Exponential smoothing is worth testing alongside it in the same setting.

02

Choose LightGBM when

You forecast many items and locations, demand responds to price, promotions or events, and those drivers are known ahead of time. Global gradient-boosted models were prominent among the top entries in the M5 competition on retail item-level sales.

03

Choose neither when

Most periods are zero: intermittent-demand methods such as Croston’s or TSB are built for that. And when history is very short, a seasonal naive forecast or borrowing from similar items can beat both. Our demand forecasting page covers these cases.

How we’d choose

Set the bar, then backtest.
Choose by item group.

The choice is an empirical one. These are the steps we follow on client data before recommending a model.

01

Set a baseline

A seasonal naive forecast and exponential smoothing on the same data, next to the method you use today. Any model has to beat them to earn its complexity.

02

Profile the portfolio

We count the series, measure history length and the share of zero periods, and list which drivers are known ahead of the forecast date.

03

Backtest at the decision’s horizon

Rolling-origin backtests that use only the data available at each origin, scored with scaled errors such as MASE or RMSSE, bias and quantile loss, by item group.

04

Choose per segment & monitor

Different groups often get different models, reconciled so item, location and total forecasts add up. Accuracy and drift are monitored after go-live.

Before we start

Good questions. Straight answers.

Is LightGBM more accurate than Prophet?

Not always. Global gradient-boosted models often win when there are many related series and useful drivers such as price and promotions. On a few smooth, seasonal series with short history, Prophet or exponential smoothing is often as accurate and simpler to run. A backtest on your own data decides.

How much history does Prophet need?

For yearly seasonality, at least two full years helps it separate seasonality from trend. Weekly seasonality needs only a few months of daily data. With less than a year, no method can learn a yearly pattern from the series alone, so borrowing from similar series becomes necessary.

Can LightGBM forecast new items?

Partly. A global model can forecast an item with little history from its attributes and from similar items, which a per-series model can’t do. Quality depends on how similar the new item really is, so new items should be scored as their own group in the backtest.

Do we have to pick one?

No. Many systems use different models for different item groups, or average several models. What matters is that every candidate goes through the same backtest and that the forecasts add up across the hierarchy.

What about foundation models such as TimesFM or Chronos?

Add them to the same backtest as another candidate. They forecast without training a model per series and do well on many datasets, but support for external drivers such as price and promotions varies by model and version. Our time series article covers when they fit.
